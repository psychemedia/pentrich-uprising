Stamford Mercury

Published Friday, 24 July 1812

NOTTINGHAM ASSIZES.

At these assizes, which commenced Thursday the 16th, the following-prisoners took their trials:—

In the County Gaol:— *Miles Lake* (aged 35), and *Benjamin Renshaw* (aged 34), were found guilty of stealing from the house of James Finch, frame-work-knitter, Mansfield, the 7th May last, upwards of 30*l.* in money; and received sentence of *death*; Lake was afterwards reprieved, for execution. [Joseph Wright (aged 20), was admitted evidence the above case; and Thomas Revill (aged 22), was also admitted evidence on another indictment against Renshaw, for having, on Saturday evening in the summer of 1810, stolen a sheep from close the parish Mansfield, the property of Isaac Dodsley.]—  ... *George Spray* (aged 27), for having, on the 13th of November last, destroyed a stocking-frame, the parish of Sutton Ashfield, the property of F. Betts, was sentenced to *fourteen years' transportation*. ... *Joseph Falconbridge* (aged 50), charged with having, on the 13th November last, destroyed a stocking-frame, the property of George Ward, of Sutton in Ashfield, was acquitted.— Against *John Bonnington* (aged 24), charged with damaging a stocking-frame at Sutton in Ashfield *no bill* was found.— *William Godfrey* (aged 35), charged with having (in company with divers other persons at present unknown) destroyed and damaged seven frames at Bulwell, the night the 10th November, was ordered *to remain till next assizes*.

Nottingham Review

Published Friday, 24 July 1812

TO DO  - opening address

Joseph Falconbridge, aged 39, of Bulwell, in this neighbourhood, stood indicted for being concerned in the well known frame-breaking riots at Sutton-in-Ashfield, in this county, on the 13th of last November. George Ward deposed, that he is a framework-knitter at Sutton-in-Ashfield aforesaid; and that on the evening of the day named in the indictment, about forty persons, some in smock frocks and other disguises, and some having black faces, came to his house, armed with sticks, *mauls*, (mallets, we suppose,) hammers, guns, &c. and that they broke two frames, and chopt the wood-work of another, his own property, while he stood in a corner of the house in a very terrified state. He further stated that they ate his bread—cheese he had none; times being as hard with him as with other people—that they demanded a candle, when he replied, I had as lieve you took my life as broke my frames; and that they remained in the house about twenty minutes, but that he did not know the prisoner. On his cross-examination by Mr. Copley, he stated that the two frames were wide ones; that one was for making lace, and the other made three spider hose at once with selvages; and that he had never made any cut-up hose, for he hated the practice as bad as any body.

George Hibbert deposed, that on the night named in the indictment, he was in Naylor's house when the mob appeared; that he was so affrighted that he fled into Ward's shop for safety, but to his great terror, in flying from danger he ran into it; that he never saw the prisoner before, nor till three months after, but was sure as to his person, though he could not tell how he was dressed; and that he saw him work hard at breaking Ward's frames with a bar of iron during the space of half an hour.

The prisoner had a good character given him in Court, and the Jury, after about ten minutes consultation, pronounced him Not Guilty.

George Spray, aged 27, a native of Hucknal Torkard, was also indicted for framebreaking on the 13th of last November.

Francis Betts stated himself to be a hosier, miller, &c. of Sutton-in-Ashfield, and that about five o'clock in the day named in the indictment, he observed, twenty, thirty, or forty persons about the mill, whom he understood wanted to speak with him. One of them asked him whether he were willing to give up his wide frames to be broken, to which he answered No—hoped they would not break his frames or any other persons; to which they replied, they had begun and should go forwards—said they had been informed he intended to fire on them, but if he did they would destroy his house and mill, and all he had—they had two thousand brave men, and if any body *ost* (attempted) to hinder them they would blow up the town. Witness heard the report of a gun—heard a great noise—heard them call for the hammer-men, and heard them *smashing* the door, then went into a close at some distance for his own safety—heard them *smashing* an hour and a half—there was much light in the house as though it was on fire—heard a gun go off, and the mob frequently shouted "pilots, colliers, and hammer-men"—saw them go to the storehouse—heard them shout for the hammer-men to open the door, after which they made a great noise with thumping it—was afraid they had seen him; and when he went to the storehouse he found the door and windows broken in, and seven frames broken—all *smashed* to pieces, and the wood-works chopt up—three of them were his own, three belonged to Mrs. Taylor, and one to Wilson. Witness found parts of the frames in the house, and other parts i'th *closin*—his loss was 400*l.* upon the whole near 600*l.*; said he never saw the prisoner there, nor did he know him.

Sally Betts, wife of the foregoing witness, deposed to all the material parts of her husband's testimony; said the mob consisted of a thousand persons, but knew nothing about the prisoner.

Mary Robinson declared herself to be a Stocking-maker, at Sutton-in-Ashfield, and that about five o'clock in the evening of the 13th of November, she saw the prisoner in the mob at Bett's house, with an iron bar in his hand. She knew him from having lived at Hucknal; saw him and several more hammer-men go to the door of the old house and break it open; saw him go in and break some frames which lay down on the floor, though she did not know whether they were wide or narrow ones; and saw him throw parts of them out of the window; after which he and several more of the mob came to her father's house and begged some tobacco. On her cross-examination she said, she had not been taken before the Magistrates herself as a frame-breaker; but admitted she had been pretty merry among them. On being asked whether she had *no other means* of obtaining a livelihood but that of stocking-making, she, with a significant smile upon her face, answered :not that I know of." The Judge interposed, and she left the box.

On the part of the prisoner, John Wass, a bricklayer, deposed, that on the evening named in the indictment, the prisoner came to his house, in Sutton-in-Ashfield, at a quarter before six, who declared that he had been compelled to come along with the mob at the risk of his life, but that he had left them as soon as he came into the town. Witness further stated, that the prisoner staid in his house till after eight, and that he then went a gatewards with him on the road home four or five hundred yards beyond Betts's house.

Jonathan Gregory, a Farmer on Sutton Forest, stated, that on the night the frames were broken, he met the prisoner going home about two miles from Bett's house.

Thomas Hardstaff, a Butcher, stated, that on going on the road from Sutton towards Hucknall, about half past [?] the afternoon on the 13th November, he met the prisoner among a considerable number of persons whom witness did not know, that the prisoner declared he had been forced from his home by the mob, from whom he, in vain, attempted to escape. And, on the witness interposing in behalf of the prisoner, they declared they would not let him depart; and that if the witness did not desist, they would split his skull with a hatchet.

The foregoing witnesses, and various others of equal respectability, gave the prisoner an excellent character, whom they described as a good husband and father, and a hard-working man. The Judge, however, informed the Jury, that character was not to weigh with them in cases like this, except they had good reason to doubt the testimony of the identifying witness or witnesses; and they pronounced him guilty, and the Judge immediately sentenced him to fourteen years' transportation.

Miles Lake, aged 35, Benjamin Renshaw, aged 31, Joseph Wright, aged 20, and Thomas Revill, aged 22, all of Mansfield, were next put to the bar; the three former were charged with having stolen a club-box, belonging to a friendly society of that place, containing upwards of 30*l.* in money or cash notes, from the house of James Finch, of Mansfield, on the 7th of March last; Renshaw, also along with Revill, stood charged with having slaughtered and carried away a three-sheer ram in the month of June, 1810, and likewise, with having wilfully set fire to a stack of hay on the 21st of April, in the same year, belonging to Mr. Charles Stanton, of Mansfield aforesaid. Wright was admitted King's evidence against Lake, about the club-box, who was found guilty both of burglariously entering the house and feloniously stealing the property, partly from his own confession at the time he was arrested, and received sentence of death; and Revill was admitted evidence against Renshaw both on account of the sheep-stealing and setting fire to the stack; of both crimes he was found guilty, and received sentence of death.

In order to give the reader a better idea of the discovery of some of these atrocities, it will be necessary to state, that suspicion having fallen upon Lake and Renshaw about the club-box, they were taken into custody, confessed the crime, and restored the property. Renshaw was then asked if he knew any thing of the other crimes named in the indictment, when he confessed, and Revill was taken into custody; and in consequence of him being considered the least guilty of the two, he was admitted evidence against his companion in iniquity.

Renshaw when first brought to the bar, pleaded guilty to both indictments with which he was charged, but was advised to put himself upon his trial; and after the most impassioned agonies for a human being to labour under, he was induced to comply with the instruction of the Court; and when he was finally brought up for trial, he swooned away before the Court could proceed.—What pangs of conscience were these!!!—It may fairly be concluded, that every one who saw the wretched state to which this miserable man was reduced, must shudder at the idea of treading in a like unhappy path, and we trust his case will operate to prevent the commission of such crimes in future.

...

John Slaney was indicted for a misdemeanor for having, at his house in the neighbourhood of Basford, on the 27th of last January, attempted to excite Edward Green, a private in the Berkshire Militia, to break a stocking frame in his, the prisoner's house. Edward Green deposed, that, on the day named in the indictment, he, along with Alexander Murford, another private in the same regiment, was stationed in Slaney's house to protect a frame which was intended, as he understood, to be fetched away to Nottingham, for fear of its being broken. He further stated, that he had not been in the workshop more than an hour before the prisoner came in and said to him, while pointing to a bar of iron which lay on the floor, "that is *Ned*, take it up and smash it," meaning a particular frame which he pointed out, and further said, "nobody will tell of you," to which witness himself replied, "no, I was sent to protect the frame, and not to break it;" To this proposition witness stated that he refused to comply; that Slaney shortly after went out of the house; that he and his comrade were relieved a short time by two constables, and that when they returned the frame was broken. On his cross examination witness denied that much laughing and joking took place between him and the prisoner, for the latter "was rather snappish." The other soldier deposed to the principle parts of the foregoing, and added, that Slaney laughed and talked a little; and a constable produced a bar of iron, which Green said was the identical *Ned* pointed at by Slaney.

On the part of the prisoner, Robert Staton deposed that he was at Slaney's house when the soldiers were there; that he heard several familiar sentences pass between Slaney and Green; that the latter said, "I wish the frame was broken;" and that they were joking about the bar, and said, "it would make a good Ned Lud's hammer."

James Casher stated that he went to Slaney's house at the time the soldiers were there; that they and the prisoner were joking about the iron bar; and that Green said Slaney ought to be damned if he did not break the frame soon.

An apprentice boy of Slaney's was called, who stated that much laughing and talking took place between his master and the soldiers; but on his cross examination he admitted that when he was in prison along with his master, a short time after the frame was broken, that the latter said to him "dont say nought."

The prisoner had a good character given him, and Counsellor Denman made a most brilliant and argumentative speech in his defence, in which he endeavoured to prove the improbability that Slaney should so far commit himself to a stranger, and that stranger a soldier, who was sent to protect the frame, in the presence too of another soldier, as to recommend to such soldier, in a serious manner, the breaking of the very frame he was sent to protect. He stated too that the frame which was said to have been pointed at by Slaney, was not proved to have been the frame that was broken in his house; therefore that the alleged crime had not been proved in point of fact, as well as its commission rendered improbable, as connected with the circumstances of the case.

Mr. Reynolds shortly replied on the part of the Crown; the Jury found the prisoner guilty, and the Judge sentenced him to three years imprisonment in the county jail.

William Godfrey, of Arnold, stood committed for the Assizes on a charge of framebreaking, at Bulwell, in the night between the 10th and 11th of last November; but on account of the absence of John Amos, the principal, and, we understand, the only material witness against him, the Grand Jury could not find a bill. But to prevent his liberation, without a trial, Mr. Sculthorpe, the prosecuting attorney, made affidavit in Court, stating, that, to the best of his belief, the said John Amos had absented himself that he might not appear against the prisoner. The affidavit then prayed that his Lordship would be pleased to order the detention of the said William Godfrey until the next Assizes. His Lordship enquired whether the prisoner had any Counsel; and, on being answered in the negative, he ordered the prisoner to be detained according to the prayer of the affidavit.

Christabel Ogle, William Stanley, Gervas Copley, Miles Lake, and Benjamin Renshaw were then put to the bar, on whom, after a most impressive lesson from his Lordship as to the nature and enormity of their several offences, he passed the awful sentence of the law, that they be taken to the place from whence they came, and then to the place of execution to hang by the neck till their bodies be dead.

Before his Lordship left the town he was pleased to reprieve the whole of them except Renshaw, whom we hope is now earnestly seeking for that mercy from an offended God, which the administrators of the violated laws of our country seem to think themselves not justified in dispensing in his favor. And we heartily congratulate the inhabitants of Mansfield on the breaking up of a gang that threatened the destruction of every species of their property.
