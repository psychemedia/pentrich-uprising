The Leveller Who Died Twice#

Ledbury, 1746. September. The land is being enclosed, and the roads too: acts of parliament are making the previously free rights of way subject to tolls, supposedly to cover the costs of bringing the roads up to scratch. But everyone knows the money’s just being used line pockets, not pay for any improvements. And even if it was — it’s still a tax. A tax that didn’t used to be there, and now it is. And there’s no more money coming in, but there is more to pay out, and the folk aren’t happy.

Since the early 1730s, turnpike gates around Bristol and Gloucester had been subject to repeated attacks. As well as the acts for setting up the new turnpikes and toll roads, an act had been introduced to make it a felony to destroy a turnpike gate or toll house; which is to say, a crime punishable by death, a crime punishable by death without benefit of clergy. Which meant if you were a vicar and joined in the affray, you couldn’t play your get out of civilian jail free card by saying you were only subject to canon law and could only be tried in an ecclesiastical court.

August 1735#

At the start of August, 1735, new turnpike gates were erected on the roads out of Ledbury. It only took a week or two for the letters to start appearing encouraging folk to join in an action of tearing down the turnpike gates. Seven or eight of the town commissioners, hearing of the plan, thought they’d meet force with force, and mustered several of their own men, stationing them on the road out towards Hereford, at the Home End turnpike.

Thomas Reynolds, aged just twenty eight, was one of those who would take up the call to bring the turnpikes down. The son of a farmer who’d put him to school, but not to a trade, he’d taken on farm work a few years before, when his parents had died. An honest and hard working young man, he was perhaps hopeful of marrying and taking on a farm of his own. I’ve heard it said that on the day in question, he’d gone into Ledbury to buy a Pair of Stockings, a pair of Shoes and a hat. And maybe he’d had a drink or two.

It’s late afternoon, turning to evening. Five o’clock. Six o’clock. People are milling around, and a sense of tense anticipation is in the air. By seven o’clock, a small crowd has gathered. A small crowd armed with guns. And axes. The light starts to fade — this is before the days of daylight savings, and British Summer time, remember. Some people are in disguise: faces blacked, men in women’s clothing. Maybe some of the folk have had a drink or two. One of the Commissioners of the Peace walks towards the group, reads the Riot Act out by candlelight: the mob now have an hour to disperse, or they’ll be arrested. So do they do as they’d been told, and head off back home? No. Quite the opposite. The plan had to be do the attack later — more people are still expected to arrive — but the starting gun has been fired. Almost literally. You can imagine the scene, two lines facing off, a few scuffles breaking out. Two of the crowd, axes in hand, their faces blacked, disguised in women’s clothing, are grabbed and taken behind the authority line. A short gun battle begins. There’s firing from both sides, but this wasn’t the plan, the guns were there to intimidate as much as anything — and the crowd members run off.

The two prisoners are taken to one of the Commissioner’s houses, John Skypp’s. He’ll hold them there securely till morning, then pack them off to jail.

But the crowd is building again. By midnight, two hundred or so, more than enough to pull down the several turnpikes. And so they do. That job done, you can perhaps imagine two or three dozen of the rebels still milling around the Market House. “But what about Thomas? What about the men they arrested?” A woman’s voice, Elizabeth Walters, is working the crowd, chivvying, harrying: “we can’t just leave them there? We can’t just let Skipp keep them locked up? We have to get them out. We have to get him back.”

It was one in the morning, Sunday morning, when they got to the house, shouting out their demands: “Give us them back or we’ll pull your house down!” and “Give us them back or we’ll burn your stables.” “Fire at the house”, screams Elizabeth Walters, and musket balls smash through the windows to show they meant it. But Skypp was expecting this, his blunderbusses and fowling pieces are primed and ready to fire. One of the rioters is killed, several others are wounded. Grabbing the body of the dead man, the rioters withdraw. And the following morning, at 11 o’clock or so, the two prisoners are sent off to Hereford gaol under a strong guard.

December 1735#

A couple of months later, and the two men are still in Hereford gaol.

It’s December, 1746, at the King’s Head Inn, just after the Ross Fair, and twenty or more of the Turnpike Cutters, or Turnpike Levellers, as they’ve come to be called, stride up to Thomas Ireland, Keeper of the jail at Hereford, demanding of him: “Why are you still keeping them then? Why don’t you let them out?” Without giving him a chance to answer, they drag him out of the Inn and into the Street, still in his boots and spurs, knocking him down then picking up, and then knocking him down again, and again, and. The Inn Keeper and his Servants try to intervene, but they’re set upon for their trouble. The Jail Keeper’s now dragged down to Wilton Bridge and the Turnpike Levellers threaten to throw him into the River; bit instead, they drag him 4 miles or so to a public house in Horewithey. He’s beaten again, and they threaten to hang him there and then if he doesn’t write a discharge letter to the Turnkey at Hereford. Four of the Jail Keeper’s colleagues from Hereford have followed them from them Ross: “come on, let him go, you’ve had your fun”, but they’re set upon in turn and have to flee for their own lives.

Six more hours they keep him there, the Jail keeper, before ferrying him over the river, and walking him about the countryside till nearly 4 in the morning. Then they robbed him of his purse — no reason not to, I guess — and some of them set off, to Hereford presumably, though I’ve not seen any reports that a prison break was ever attempted, leaving a few others to stand guard over the Jail keeper. But they weren’t the best of jailers. Before long, they started bickering and fighting over who should get what share of the money they’d stolen from him, and as they quarrelled and fought with each other, the Jailer made his own escape.

February 1736#

Winter turns to Spring, and in advance of the Worcester Spring Assizes, at the start of April 1736, several proclamations are advertised reminding folk of the terms of the Riot Act, the Black Act, and the Act against destroying turnpikes. A reward of £100 is offered for the discovery and apprehension of several named persons, £100 each, including William Bithell, a labourer from Ledbury, and Elizabeth Walters, a servant woman, as well as others unknown, implicated in the destruction of the Ledbury turnpikes, the attempt to free the two prisoners, the laws against riotous assembly, the attack on the Hereford Jail keeper and the making of threats against persons or property in cases relating to the Ledbury turnpike riots.

With at least one person turning King’s evidence, Francis Rawlins, the Miller, several persons did stand trial at the Worcester Assizes. William Bithel was one of them, William Morgan another, Thomas Hale a third. Those three were capitally convicted for cutting down the turnpikes. But not before a crowd had gathered the morning of the trial outside the Judge’s lodgings, and a troupe of soldiers had had to attend to disperse a mob.

A couple of weeks later, with Hale’s sentence commuted to one of transportation, Bithell and Morgan were hanged. Expecting trouble — there were rumours that Forest men would come and do derring do feats of bravery, Robin Hood style, freeing the condemned men — eighty soldiers and a captain had been brought in to police the event; but the crowd was quiet — unusually so — largely made up of married women who’d persuaded their men folk to remain at home.

At the end of May, the two men who’d been taken prisoner right at the start of the Ledbury turnpike riots, James Baylis and Thomas Reynolds, were taken to Newgate prison in London.

They were tried in early July under the terms of the Black Act, for going in Disguise, armed with Offensive Weapons. Now, I’ve heard it said that Reynolds said he’d had nothing to do with the attack, that the magistrates men had attacked him, knocked him to the ground, where he’d got covered with dirt — and it was dirt and dust, not a blacked face of disguise — that he was being mistakenly charged for. But I’ve also heard it said he’d maybe had more than a pint or two, and had been leading the charge with axe in hand, wanting to be the first to break the turnpike. But whatever the case, after a Trial of just four hours, Baylis and Reynolds were found guilty and remanded back to Newgate. A week later, they were brought back for sentencing: death.

At some point, Baylis’ wife had followed him up to London (I’ve no idea whether their two or three young children went up with her), and taken lodgings in a public inn. At some point, she’d fallen sick whilst she was there, so ill she’d feared for her own life at one point, but she seems to have either fully recovered, or recovered enough, to do what she could for her husband in his death row cell. As the story tells it, she begged everyone she could to plea for a reprieve for her husband. And whether it was her pleas, or their pleas, or some other reason altogether, I don’t know, but I do know that Baylis would get a reprieve two or three days before he was due to be hanged, his sentence reduced to 14 years transportation.

But Thomas Reynolds, just twenty eight years of age, a young farmer with his life ahead of him, hopeful of marriage, and his own farm, perhaps, would not be so fortunate: he would pay the ultimate price, at Tyburn, at the end of July, at the end of a rope.

The day before his execution, he gives a small amount of money to Baylis’ wife. “For a shroud, and a coffin.”

After hanging the usual time, Jack Catch, the Executioner, cut him down. Justice served, the Sheriff’s men departed, and Reynolds body was placed in the simple coffin. But as the Coffin was being fastened down — fffwwwwhhhhh — and Reynolds thrust back the Lid. He clapped his hands on the sides of the coffin, and tried to raise himself up. To say the crowd was astonished was only the half of it. Suspecting foul play — people had tried to find ways of tricking the executioner before, and would try after — and he was all for trying to hang Reynolds again, though others thought he should just be knocked on the head and disposed of that way; but the Mob weren’t having any of it and amidst shout of “Save his life”, they set upon the executioner and secreted Reynolds away, eventually taking him to to a House a mile or two away. But he wasn’t in a good state. After vomiting three Pints of Blood, or so the records say, folk were still hopeful that he’d survive, and they gave him a medicinal glass of wine; but that appears to have been one drink too many, and Thomas Reynolds died for a second time.
