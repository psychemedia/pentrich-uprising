Morning Chronicle

Published Tuesday, 3 August 1802

BATH, AUGUST 2.

The following account of outrages lately committed will shew the distracted state of the Clothing Districts in this neighbourhood.

The discontents first manifested themselves about the beginning of April last, by cutting off the young trees of several manufacturers of Bradford, Westbury, and Warminster; and by a variety of anonymous letters, conceived in terms the most horrible, threatening fire and destruction to the clothiers.

April 22.— A large hay-rick belonging to Mr. Warren, of Warminster, manufacturer, was set on fire in the night, but being discovered, was extinguished without much damage.

In May soon after, a dog-kennel and a large oat-rick of Mr. Warren's was set on fire in the night, and totally consumed.

June 12.—The cart of Mr. Bayley, of Calstone Mills, near Devizes, returning from Warminster loaded with cloths, was overtaken on the Downs by six men with blackened, faces, who took out the cloths, cut five or six of them to pieces, and damaged 14 others, to the amount of 200l.

June 23.— A gun was fired in the dead of night into the window of a house where a workman of Messrs. Bleeck and Strode, manufacturers, at Warminster, lodged, but missed him by about 7 inches. The bullets went through the house, across the street, and into a house on the other side of the road. Another rick set on fire at Trowbridge; the property of Mr. Dunn, manufacturer, but happily extinguished. Another rick set on fire at Bradford, and totally consumed, the property of Mr. Tugwell, manufacturer, who had also received a threatening Letter.

June 29.— An assemblage of workmen from Trowbridge, Westbury, and other towns, in the evening at Warminster; and about one o'clock in the night, a gun was fired into the chamber of Mr. Henry Wansey, manufacturer, but happily did no mischief except breaking the windows.

July 6.— In the dead of night a gun was fired into the house of John Jones, and another, in a minute after into the house of ——— Baker, both workmen of Mr. Henry Wansey, which missed them and their children by a few inches only. A gun fired at the house of Mr. Stancombe, manufacturer, at Trowbridge; but the bullets striking against the wall, flattened and fell down.

July 13.— Though the Magistrates were sitting at Warminster, at the general quarter-sessions for the county, yet on this night a barn, stable, &c. the property of Mr. G. Warren, manufacturer, of Warminster, was set on fire, and totally consumed.— An anonymous Letter came by post to another manufacturer of Warminster, threatening, by Almighty God to have his life within a month.

July 14. This night the mill of Messrs. Jones and Co. manufacturers, at Staverton, near Bradford, was attacked by an armed body of men, who fired in a regular manner near 100 rounds of bullets, slugs, &c. which demolished the windows and did other damage; but the assailants were obliged to desist without accomplishing their purpose, leaving behind them a pick-axe, part of a powder-horn, and powder and shot.

July 16.— This night the large pile of buildings at Clifford Mills, near Beclsington, the property of Mr. Newton, manufacturer of Stoke, were set on fire, and totally destroyed.— The damage is estimated at nearly 1500l. Great part might have been saved, but the populace would not permit it.

July 21.— The Mills at Littleton, the property of Mr. Naish, manufacturer, at Trowbridge, were attacked by an armed body of men with blackened faces, set on fire, and totally consumed; they went away, threatening destruction to other mills. The loss here is estimated at 800l.

July 24.— This night two ricks, the property of Mr. Newman, of Melksham, was set on fire, and great part consumed.

We are extremely concerned to find that these alarming riots and lawless acts have not yet entirely subsided, notwithstanding every precaution is taken to put an end to such outrages. Detachments of troops have gone from all the neighbouring towns, and every manufactory is strictly guarded, yet the enthusiasm of those misguided people is carried to such an excess, that an Wednesday night an armed body of them attacked the factory at Staverton, belonging to Messrs. Jones, Hart, and Jones, and fired from three different parts, at the centinels on duty, who returned the fire, but hearing the horse patrole coming up the depredators retreated. And on Thursday night a dwelling-house situated at Bearfield, near Bradford, the property of John Jones, Esq. one of the partners in the above firm, was destroyed by fire, and there is every reason to suppuose that it was wilfully done. The premises were unoccupied.

Thus are the workmen proceeding in their outrages, and in defiance of all law are attempting to dictate to their masters, and carry their points by intimidations and violence; an attempt which not only manufacturers are highly interested to withstand, but it is also the interest and duty of all classes to prevent, because it aims at establishing a precedent which would overturn the very foundation on which all civilised society is constructed. —*Bath Journal.*

The Magistrates, having expressed their great abhorrence at the outrageous and disgraceful proceedings that have lately taken place in various parts of this county, by tumultuous assemblies of persons, some of whom have appeared in disguise, and the burning and destroying of different Manufactories, and other property, and threatening the lives of individuals, came to the following Resolution:—

That they are determined to take the most effectual and vigorous measures for bringing the offenders to speedy justice; and for that purpose they will hold daily meetings, at twelve o'clock, at the following places, viz.

Thursday and Saturday, TROWBRIDGE;  
Tuesday WARMINSTER;  
Friday MELKSHAM;  
Monday and Wednesday BRADFORD.

AT numerous and respectable MEETING of the WOOLLEN-MANUFACTURERSof  the WEST of ENGLAND, holden this 16th day of August, 1802, at the White-Hart Inn, Bath, pursuant to adjournment:—

*Mr. JOHN JONES, jun. in the Chair;*

After having taken into consideration the many OUTRAGES which have recently been committed by some deluded Workmen, who ignorantly imagine themselves to injured by the use of certain Machinery, and have in consequence reported very unfounded and erroneous accusations against their Masters for its introduction, whereby the public mind may unwarily have been influenced to commiserate their representations; and the Meeting being duly impressed with the conviction that the produce of a man's ingenuity (as well in the woollen as in other manufactures and agriculture) is as sacred a species of property as any other estate whatsoever:—

IT WAS UNANIMOUSLY RESOLVED,

That the Subscription entered into at the last Meeting, for the purpose of offering Rewards for the discovery of, and bringing to conviction, the offenders, who have been or shall hereafter be guilty of such outrages, and for the defraying the expences of prosecutions, shall be promoted by every means in the power of this Meeting.

That the Chairman be desired to caution the Public against the scandalous and false reports circulated by the Sheermen and others; and also to caution all persons against illegal contributions, subscriptions, and combinations.

That, *for the preservation of the trade of this part of the country*, it is the determination of this Meeting to defend the Machinery already introduced, and any which from its utility, may be judged advisable hereafter to introduce into the woollen-manufacture, against the attacks of any person or persons whomsoever; the Manufacturers engaging to find employ for all persons in their respective employment, in some other branch of the manufacture, of which such person shall at capable, at ample and sufficient wages, in case by the introduction of machinery the services of any such persons shall be rendered unnecessary in the particular branch of the manufacture in which they are now or have lately been employed; so long such persons shall demean themselves with propriety.

The Chairman having reported to the Meeting, that a Subscriber having (in consequence of a promise of a Reward of Five Hundred Pounds, to be paid out of the Fund subscribed by this Meeting) obtained information against several persons concerned in setting fire to LITTLETON MILLS; and that some of such offenders have in consequence thereof been apprehended and committed; RESOLVED, That on the conviction of any one or more of such offenders so committed, the said reward of Five Hundred Pounds be paid by the Chairman out of the fund established by this Meeting.

That the Chairman, with any four other Gentlemen he may nominate, be appointed a Committee to examine the several Statutes relating to the Woollen. Manufactures, and report abstracts of their contents at the next Meeting.

That a reward of Fifty Pounds shall paid to the first person (whether an accomplice or not,) who shall discover the perpetrators, aiders, or abettors, concerned in any malicious felony that may hereafter be committed against the property or personal safety of any of the Manufacturers who subscribe to the fund established by this Meeting, or of any of their workmen or persons employed in their defence; to be paid on conviction of the offender or offenders. And that any person or persons who shall apprehend and deliver to the Civil Power any offender or offenders, who shall be assembled together for the purpose of committing depredations on the property of any of the Subscribers, shall receive Five Guineas reward for each person so apprehended; such last-mentioned reward to be paid on the commitment of such offender or offenders.

That George Hayward Tugwell, Esq. banker, in the city of Bath, be appointed Treasurer of the Funds belonging to this Meeting, with power to make calls upon the Subscribers, under the direction of the Chairman, who is hereby authorised to make good from such funds any rewards offered, upon the conviction of any offender or offenders; and to defray the expences of prosecutions, according to the resolutions of the 2d of August last.

That these Resolutions be advertised in the Star and Sun, and in the Bath Chronicle, Bath Herald, Rudhall's and Pine's Bristol, Salisbury, and Glocester Newspapers; and that copies be printed and circulated in the manufacturing neighbourhoods.

That this Meeting be adjourned to Monday the 13th day of September next, subject to an earlier call by the Chairman. Signed, on behalf of the Meeting, JOHN JONES, jun. Chairman.

OUTLINE OF THE LATE ACT OF PARLIAMENT, FOR THE PRESERVATION OF THE HEALTH AND MORALS OF APPRENTICES AND, OTHERS, IN COTTON OR OTHER MILLS AND FACTORIES.

The Act recites, that it has of late became a practice, in such Mills and Factories, to employ a great number of *Male* and *Female* Apprentices in the same building; in consequence of which, Regulations are become necessary to preserve the health and morals of the persons so employed; and it therefore enacted, that from the *2d. of December, 1802*, all such Mills and Factories within Great Britain, and, Ireland, wherein *three* or more such *Apprentices*, or *twenty* or more *other persons* are employed, shall be subject to the Regulations contained in the Act; of which the following is a short abstract or epitome.

1st. All the apartments to be *washed with quick lime* and water, twice at least every year; and due attention paid to the supply of *fresh air* in such Mills and Factories. 2d. Every Apprentice to have two complete suits of *clothing*; one new complete suit being supplied, once least in every year.— 3d. No Apprentice to *work* more than twelve hours a day, exclusive of meal time; and (after the the 1st of June, 1803, and with the exception aftermentioned) never to work between nine at night and six in the morning. 4th. When 1000 splindles, or more are employed, *night-work* may be continued, for a further period of six (or in certain cases - twelve) months. 5th. Every Apprentice to have daily one lesson in *reading*, *writing*, or *arithmetic*, from a proper person to be provided and paid by the Master; 6th. The *sleeping-rooms* of the Male Apprentices to be distinct from those of the Female : and not more than two Apprentices to sleep in one bed. 7th. Every Apprentice to be instructed one hour at least every Sunday in the principles of the *Christian Religion*; and, if its parents be of the Church of England, to be examined once a year by the Rector, &c. of the Parish; and between the age of fourteen and eighteen years, to be prepared for confirmation, and brought to the Bishop to be, confirmed : or, if in Scotland, and of that Church, to be examined once a year by the Minister; and between the age of fourteen and eighteen years, to be carried to that Church, to receive the Sacrament : and the Master to take care that all his Apprentices do attend divine worship regularly every Sunday. 8th. Visitors (being either Magistrates or Clergymen of the Established Church) to be appointed at the Midsummer Sessions, *to visit* such Mills and Factories, and to report to the Quarter Sessions the state and condition thereof. 9th. In case of the prevalence of an *infectious disorder* in any such Mill or Factory, the visitor to require the Master to call in a Physician, or other competent medical person, to take measures for preventing the spreading of the infection; the expence of such medical assistance to be discharged by the Master of such Mill or Factory. 10th. Any person *opposing* or molesting *the Visitors*, to forfeit for every such offence, a sum not more than 10l. nor less than 5l. 11th. Printed or written *Copies of the Act* to be put up in two or more conspicuous places in every such Mill or Factory. 12th. The Master of every such Mill or Factory to forfeit for every *wilful offence* against this Act (except as aforesaid), a sum not more than 5l. nor less than 40s.; the application of which, and the mode and form of conviction (the information being to be laid within one calendar month), and the manner of levying the penalties, are next specified in the Act. 13th. Every such Master, yearly at the Epiphany Sessions, to enter every such Mill or Factory with the *Clerk of the Peace*, who shall have a fee of 2s. for every such entry.

Salisbury and Winchester Journal

Published Monday, 14 March 1803

At the Assizes for the county of Wilts, which began here on Monday last, Thomas Hilliker `[sic]`, a workman in the clothing business, was tried before Mr. Baron Thompson, on a capital indictment, charging him with having riotously assembled with divers other persons, and wilfully aided in setting fire to Littleton Mill, in the parish of Steeple Ashton. The trial was an object of great interest to the manufacturers and their workmen, particularly to the shearmen and weavers, the setting fire to the mill having been one of those outrages which arose out the dispute respecting the of machinery, &c. and it was effected by seven men, entered the mill armed, and with their faces blackened, and whilst two of them stood centinel over Mr. Ralph Heath, the Overseer of the Mill, threatening him with instant death if he endeavoured to prevent their purpose, the others went up stairs, and deliberately set fire to the building. A very numerous body of their associates supposed to have been assembled in the neighbourhood, and to have detached these men on this dangerous service.—Messrs. Gibbs and Dallas conducted the prosecution; Mr. Garrow was engaged for the prisoner on a special retainer, with Messrs. Jekyll and Burrough.—Mr. Heath was positive Hilliker was the second who entered the factory; he wis near him a considerable time, so that he had opportunity of observing his whole person; and another witness proved that Mr. Heath, on afterwards seeing Hilliker, recognized him as the man, and was much agitated thereby, Mr. Garrow endeavoured to shew that the men who entered the factory were disguised, it was impossible for any one to know their features; and he cross-examined Mr. Heath, whether he had not confessed that he could not swear to the prisoner. Mr. Heath said, his face was not so black as to hide his features, and that he had said he did not know the prisoner from a dread that his life was in danger, if it had been understood that he could convict him.—The Jury deliberated for about teh minutes, during which time Hilliker was in great agitation; when they at length pronounced him *Guilty*, he appeared as if relieved from a suspense, and to assume degree of fortitude to encounter his fate, and which he retained when sentence of death was pronounced. Published Saturday, 14 May 1881

History of Trowbridge. [BY HISTORICUS.]

THE STORY OF THOMAS HELLIKER.

In the early part of the present century, when the enquiring minds of Stevenson, Brunel, Arkwright, the Heathcotes, and many others, were introducing machinery to supersede hand labor, a great outcry was raised by the artizans who had been accustomed to complete their work by their own ingenuity, that their bread was to be taken from them and that in future men would not be required to finish work which could be executed by mechanical means. So intense was the feeling that many acts of violence were the outcome. In Nottingham and many other large towns whole buildings were wrecked and the machines they contained scattered to the winds.

One class of handicraftsmen in connection with cloth manufacturing was shearmen; a branch of the trade that required peculiar capabilities in the workers, and unique and particular they were in all that pertained to them. For the information of those readers who may not understand the work of "shear-men," we need scarcely say that their duties were to shear the face of the cloth with large appliances answering to that description of implement; and such perfection did they attain in the art of shearing, that a cloth was by that process rendered smooth as satin on the face of it. The work required considerable bodily exertion, and was necessarily a tedious performance. They were a jovial section of the community were the old shearmen, for the beer score of one shop full of men, in one week, reached 1008 quarts! They were very choice, too, in their social intercourse; for a particular part of a public-house was reserved for their accommodation, and woe betide those who pre-occupied it. The admission to the brotherhood was stringent; and, after initiation, each one's interest was identical, no matter what the sacrifice, in defence of what they considered to be their rights. Thus much to explain the terrible event we are about to relate. About 1802 new machinery began to be introduced into the cloth trade. A Mr. Nash, cloth manufacturer, had a mill in the Conigre, and one at Lyttleton, in which machinery had been placed. The shearmen, in a body, with blackened faces, sought to destroy it, first by setting fire to the mill in Trowbridge. Thus emboldened they went to Lyttleton. Amongst the party was a man who had peculiarly projecting front teeth. He carried a horse pistol. Arrived at Lyttleton, the watchman, Heath, was called out, and coolly ordered to clear out himself and family, unless he wished to be burnt alive, as they (the shearmen) were come to burn down the mill and its contents. With the formidable horse pistol presented at his head, Heath was only too glad to comply with their orders, stood off at some distance with his family, and was compelled to watch the destruction of his master's mill, powerless to prevent it. Next morning saw Heath in Trowbridge, in company with the constables, in search of the perpetrators of the previous night's outrage. He could not be mistaken; he should be sure to identify the man who held the pistol to his head, because of his projecting front teeth. In Stilman and Noad's shop he came upon a youth of 18 or 19, named Thomas Helliker, who, unfortunately, had projecting front teeth. "That's the man that held the pistol at me," said Heath, and notwithstanding his earnest protestations to the contrive, he was seized, tried at the Spring Assize at Salisbury, found guilty, and sentenced to death, judge and jury believing the witness, Heath, that the youth Helliker was the real culprit who threatened to shoot him. Helliker was an apprentice, and was what was then known as a "shear colt." Willing or unwilling, he was forced to join the band of midnight marauders, who, with blackened faces, fired the mill at Lyttleton; but he protested to the last that he never had a pistol, nor took any part in the dark deeds of that fatal night; but he knew who the real culprit Was; and he was a man with projecting front teeth. Helliker was taken to execution, the rope placed round his neck, he ascended the steps to the scaffold, and stood beneath the beam. Even at that awful moment he was implored by the High Sheriff to confess who the perpetrators of these deeds were, and he was led off the scaffold with the rope still round his neck, in the hope that he would divulge the names; but he steadily refused to do so, knowing that if he did, his life would not he worth much, so stringent were the rules of this mysterious order. Asserting his innocence he was hung, a sacrifice for the rest. So profound an impression did this tragic event create throughout the order of shearmen in the cloth manufacturing districts, that the shearmen of Wilts, Somerset and Yorkshire assembled, the body was brought from Salisbury, and met outside the town by a large number of young girls dressed all in white, and an immense body of people by whom the deceased was regarded as a martyr—and martyr he certainly was, but it was martyrdom worthy a better cause. The body was interred in the Trowbridge Parish churchyard, with much and we believe sincere lamentation; and a tomb was erected over his remains, surmounted by carved work representing the shearmens' arms, supported on each side by the figures of Justice and Mercy, which upheld an urn.

During the time Helliker was in gaol he wrote several letters to his relations and some verses which were printed by Mr. Sweet, of Trowbridge. Following are literal copies:—

[*A Letter to his Mother.*]

March 13th, 1803.  
ALAS my distressed state! but I thank the Almighty God, I am more composed than I was when I saw my Brothers last. Dear Father and Mother, I wish you and my Brothers and Sisters to come here to see me before the 22d of March. Dear Father and Mother, I have been troubled with a very bad head-ake, but thanks be to the Almighty God, that I think I never found myself better than I have been for these two days past, both in mind and body, and I hope it will continue until the fatal day, then I hope the Lord will receive my departing spirit, I hope that my Brothers will be here, for I have something to say to them, so God Almighty bless you all.

*So no more from your unfortunate Son.*

THOMAS HELLIKER.

March 21st, 1803.

MY dear Brothers, I hope this will be a warning to you all, and consider my untimely fate (being cut off in the prime of life), which I hope will be a warning to all young men like me, and for the love of God Almighty, let my fate be always in your memory, to think that before I knew what life was, caught off to everlasting (I hope) happiness; I thank God I find myself very happy in mind, and I hope the Lord God will receive my soul, so no more,

*Remember my last fate,*

THOMAS HELLIKER.  
[*This he gave to his Brothers in the Prison when he saw them.*]

March 22d, 1803.

DEAR Father and Mother, Brothers and Sisters, and all relations, this is my last night; in the middle of the night I wrote this Letter; give my love to my Uncle Ebsworth, and to all the rest of his family. Dear Father and Mother, I think that some of the principal Shearmen has been the ruin of my body, (but I hope the Lord will receive my soul), and that they will put this on my Tomb-Stone to be a warning to all young men; to a warning against bad advice, and for my Brothers to be good to you both, for it is a bad thing to forget you, when you read this I hope my soul will be at rest forever and ever. Dear Father and Mother, give this to my Brother Joseph, and think of me when he swears, and I hope in God he will leave it off.

*Farewell forever and ever, Yours, &c.*

THOMAS HELLIKER

[*This he wrote the same night after he saw his Relations, and left it for them in his stocking. This was his Birth-day and he was Executed in the same hour he was Born.*]

VERSES

To thee my God and Saviour I,  
By day and night address my cry,  
Vouchsafe my mournful voice to hear,

My soul draws nigh to death's cold shade,
