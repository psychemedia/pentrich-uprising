```{admonition} Three daring acts of frame-breaking, July 1814
:class: dropdown

In *Nottingham Review*, [29 July 1814](https://www.britishnewspaperarchive.com/image-viewer?issue=BL%2F0001100%2F18140729&page=4&article=055).

It is with sincere regret that we have to record three daring acts of frame-breaking, which were perpetrated last Tuesday night. Surely the perpetrators of these deeds are governed by some demon's influence, thus to violate every principle of sound policy as well as the law. For though, as the learned Judge Bailey said, on the trial of Carnell and Maples, there is not the same degree of moral turpitude attached to the crime of frame-breaking, as there is to those crimes which are committed for the sake of plunder; yet this may ultimately lead to consequences, far more destructive in their issue, than those which are marked with a deeper shade of guilt. There is such danger too of the practice of making a forcible entrance into dwelling-houses in the night, (though that be on the assumed, yet false, ground of retributive vengeance,) rendering the crime familiar to the mind; and thereby engendering a propensity to the commission of every sort of plunder and outrage. We are justified in this remark, as well from the bias of the mind becoming demoralised by a train of disobedience to the law, as from the fact of several pieces of net having been taken away on the occasions we are about to relate; that being a practice which the frame-breakers rarely adopted in the early siege of their depredations...- Between twelve and one o'clock on Tuesday night, a party, supposed to he numerous, forced their way into the house of Mr. Hooton, of Old Snenton; and besides demolishing four excellent point net frames, belonging to Mr. T. Haines, they took away, as we are informed, about seventeen yards of net.— They then proceeded to the house of John Cooper, of New Snenton, who unfortunately happened to be ill in bed; and, after having forced the door, they went into the workshop and demolished one of the most valuable point net frames ever made, which belongs to Mr. Stenson. The frame was an extraordinary width; and will cost about 70*l.* to put in a working state. From Snenton the depredators proceeded to Commerce-row, in this town, near the Bugle Horn, to the house of one Kelsall, who works as a journeyman to Mr. J. Woodward, the latter accommodating him by letting him have his frame at home, and another for the sake of company in the shop. The frame-breakers demanded admission, as they said, for the purpose of breaking two of Mr. T. Haines's frames. In vain did the man protest, that none of Mr. Haines's frames were there; for, with imprecations of no common kind, they said they knew he spoke falsely; and therefore proceeded to demolish the frames, which they completely effected. Neither of them belonged to Mr. Haines, nor had either of them been at work at a reduced price. Not content with this spoilation, the depredators took away six yards of net, a spindle-bar, and a bobbin of silk.
```

```{admonition} To The Point Net Hands, August 1814
:class: dropdown
In *Nottingham Review*, [05 August 1814](https://www.britishnewspaperarchive.com/image-viewer?issue=BL%2F0001100%2F18140805&page=4).

TO THE POINT NET HANDS

In consequence of the unprovoked, unlawful, and scandalous Attack made on our Property, by destroying one of the completest POINT NET LACE FRAMES in the Trade, my Partners have come to the Resolution *not to make any more Point Net, at any Price, for the present;* for which I am extremely sorry, well knowing it will distress many honest and industrious Men. But at the same time I must declare, that their Attack upon us was most shameful, and must arise either through gross Misrepresentation, or base Ingratitude.— SInce we have been in the Business, we have employed many Hands who would not otherwise have had any Work, and we defy any one to say, that we have ever taken any unfair or undue Advantage of the Situation of Trade at any Time; but, on the contrary, on the happy return of Peace, in the hope of a good Trade, we very cheerfully gave such Advance of Prices as the Circumstances then appeared to justify, both in the Price of Working and Winding. But now, instead of a good Trade, we have scarcely any Trade at all.— We are of Opinion with every House in the Trade, that the Advance cannot be continued; and it is Folly and Madness to say, that *we*, or any other House, are the Cause of it— the Manufacturers are the greatest Sufferers— and from the serious Competition we have from the French Market, for all kinds of FANCY GOODS, it would be well for the Point Hands if they could obtain Work at the old Standard Price; but that will be impossible,  until the Demand *increases*. And if that happy period should arrive, nothing is truer than the Principle, that an extra Demand will always insure an extra Price.

As fas as regards my own individual Conduct towards our Workmen, I should leave it to those we have employed at Nottingham, Mansfield, and Derby, to answer.

I am, your's, &c.  
J. STENSON  
Nottingham, August 1, 1814.
```

A week after Mr. Stenson's notice that he and his partners were quitting the point net trade, a letter appeared in the *Nottingham Review* that attempted to explain the economic principles under which the manufacturers were forced to operate.

```{admonition} Framebreaking vs economics, August 1814
:class: dropdown
In *Nottingham Review*, [12 August 1814](https://www.britishnewspaperarchive.com/image-viewer?issue=BL%2F0001100%2F18140812&page=3).

On Frame Breaking

To the Editor of the Nottingham Review

Sir— It has long been my intention to address a few remarks to you, on the outrages in this town and neighbourhood, connected as those outrages have been, with the destruction of private property, the injury im many cases, of unoffending workmen, and on all occasions in violation of every principle of *law, policy, and justice*. Could frivolous impertinence and low scurrily, operate to deter from the crime of framebreaking, the columns of the Nottingham Gazette have in this way, given "line upon line and precept upon precept." The minds of men however are not to be influenced by attacks of that sort; other weapons are necessary in a warfare, which has for its object, the conquest of error.Besides, the Fribbler has invariably stated it, (for party purposes no doubt,) as connected with, and arising from the Jacobins and Bonaparteans in this town, who he, the political Knight-errant is appointed to exterminate. How eagerly he pursues the phantoms of his brain— how miserably he has harrassed the poor Sancho Panza the lawyer, by his wild enterprises, is well-known to his readers. In his transient intervals of reason however, he cannot but be aware, that the crime of framebreaking, has been impartially cruel in its operations; and that the Whig and the Tory have been equally the victims of its misplaced and ill-judged vengeance. Like the framebreaker, he indulges the most rancorous enmity, against those who wish *him* no harm, and on many occasions it has been difficult to decide, whether cruelty or cowardice has been the more predominating feature, in his attacks on private character.

Injudicious as the character of the Editor is, it can be no apology however, for measures which are subversive of every principle of justice, nay even of the very establishments of society itself. The formation of society was originally intended for the mutual defence of those who compose it; that the weak might be protected, against the attacks of the strong and the lawless. When man enters society, he delegates his individual power to the better protection of society at large, and *that* protection must be confided to the laws, which "enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong." To revenge ones own injuries, whether real or imaginary, is treading back the steps from civilization to barbarism, and were the principle universally acted upon, the country would throughout be one scene of revenge, bloodshed, and devastation. The man who takes the law into his own hands, to revenge his injuries, will be the very worst judge, as to the degree of punishment to be inflicted, and the irritation under which he labours, will generally induce him to inflict more than is due to the offender; nor will the man acting under the influence of strong anger, be likely to discriminate correctly; the innocent wil be confounded with the guilty. Nonetheless cases must happen too where the individuals are incapable, were it wise and just to requite their own injuries. A savage in the wilds of America might seize his bludgeon, and inflict destruction on the individual he considers dangerous to his security, interest, or repose, but *he* in turn must encounter the same danger; and the danger will compel him to seek some association, however limited. Hence arose the earliest beginnings of society.

Considerations of this nature, will induce a man who deliberates, to prefer the security which the laws of society afford, to the means of revenge and injury which fall within his own reach. It is true, the wisest and the best of laws, will in some respects be imperfect, nor can we expect, that even that best bulwark of our liberties, "Trial by Jury" should invariably be correct in its decisions. The administration of the laws in this country however, under that invaluable right of Britons, approximates as nearly to a perfect dispensation of justice, as was probably ever known, or as the imperfect state of human nature will allow.It has been the pride and boast of Englishmen, that they have been obedient to the laws, and less swayed by their passions than the people of most of the surrounding nations. The crime of framebreaking, and the robbery and mischief however to which it leads, are so cruel — so unmanly — so cowardly, and so ineffective, that it is derogatory and disgraceful to the character of Britons. Were the principal acted on universally, who would not feel ashamed of the name of Englishman? Nor can it admit of a question whether even the dispensation of an imperfect form of laws is not preferable to a state of CONFUSION, OUTRAGE, and ANARCHY, where every retributive punishment would be inflicted by the irritated and revengeful.

The malice of these deluded men, is directed chiefly against the employers of frames, falsely imagining it is in *their power* to raise the price of wages. I shall be able to prove, I trust, that were the manufacturer ever so much disposed, he is *unable* to maintain a high price of labour. TO DO

It will appear, I think, undeniable, that the price of labour must be affected by two circumstances, COMPETITION AND DEMAND. When a manufacturer has to contend with an individual opponent, a neighbouring manufacturer, or a foreign supply, his first consideration will be, "How are the prices of my goods, compared with those of my opponents?" If there are as low, or lower, taking quality into consideration, he may them meet them in the market with success; but if higher, he might as well attempt to stop the sun in its course, as to succeed against the common principles of interest in commercial transactions. Competition, though beneficial to the public at large, operates as to profit, unfavorably to both employer and employed. As soon, however, as it has brought the prices so low, as to afford to the employer on the one hand, insufficient profit, for his capital; and to the employed an inadequate maintenance on the other; the one will remove his capital into other channels, the other direct his labour to different quarters. Competition will thus regulate the prices the manufacturer *must* give for labour; and providing the price demanded for workmanship, be such, as to bring his goods above the prices of those of his opponents, he will be utterly incapable, were he ever so desirous, to maintain that high price of labour. Demand, is sometimes the result of competition, which by lowering excessive profits, not unfrequently brings the article within the reach of a greater number of purchasers. Employment, both for capital and labour, is *then* increased; though with less profit to individuals as before observed. It is generally more beneficial, however, as well to the particular manufactories, as to the public at large. To the one it affords increased employment; to the other a cheap supply. The demand for an article is also affected by the change of fashion, and *always* by an excessively high price, which price operates unfavourably in two ways; by diminishing the number of buyers, and by benefiting rival manufacturers. It is the natural effect of a great demand for any particular article, either of produce or manufactures, *eventually* to lower that article, by turning an increased capital towards that source of employment, though in commencement of such demand it may have had an effect directly the contrary. Hence, no doubt, has arisen the overgrown number of manufacturers in the leading trade of Nottingham; and hence has arisen the diminution of profits to the manufacturer of hosiery, which will, on the ground before stated, should the trade not alter, induce many to withdraw their capital from an employment which so ill repays the risk and application it requires. Another cause there is still which most seriously affects the demand for labour, and that is ACCURSED WAR. This is of itself a Pandora's box, from which come misery and woe in all their combination and consequences. This it is which depopulates villages, starves our manufacturers, ruins our merchants, and fills our workhouses, and this it is which has, no doubt, been the primary cause of that spirit of revenge and insubordination which unhappily prevails to the disgrace of our town. Thus, Sir, we see that the price of labour must be affected by several circumstances, which are not within the control of the employer; and that, *were he ever so much disposed, he is utterly incapable to maintain a high price of labour,* which *price is affected by the circumstances to which I have alluded.*

Violence and terror, the means resorted to by frame-breakers, are moreover so much at variance with the reciprocal independence which subsists between the employer and the employed, that it is strange men could ever be brought to act with so much injustice and impolicy. The master stands as much in need of labour, as the workman of employment, and they are reciprocally independent. If the work be objectionable to the stockinger, it would be as unjust to compel him to work it, as it would be to oblige the master to employ workmen at prices, or on qualities, which are unsuitable for his markets. Nor was it ever known that measures of violence tended to benefit the perpetrators. Eventually the mischief and disgrace must fall on their own heads, and the consequence will be, that the trade will either be considerably narrowed, or removed to a distance. In 1766, the silk trade was bad in Spitalfields, and the prices of work were consequently reduced. Riots ensued, and magistrates were empowered to fix and regulate wages from time to time, on the erroneous principle that even *any* law could compel higher wages than the state of the trade would allow. The natural consequence followed, the masters left off particular branches of their trade, and the workmen themselves eventually experienced the greatest loss, for the trade of ribbons, &c., was removed to Coventry, Leek, and other places.

To some it may appear strange, that I should have engaged so much of your paper, in attempting to prove the folly and wickedness of measures, which are so obviously flagrant. It has been my design however, particularly, to prove the impolicy an inefficacy of measures, which, however unjustifiable in their commencement, did not exhibit those deeper shades of depravity and vice, which they have latterly developed. During a period of suffering, unparalleled, (excepting perhaps by the immediate visitation of war itself), amidst all the distress which has visited the cottages of our artizans, cold and relentless must be that human being who has not most deeply bewailed the sufferings of his fellow-men; bitter indeed has been the cup of sorrow, and deeply have they drank of it. poor consolation is it too, that the manufacturer has himself borne a share in the common calamities which was has entailed upon us; a brighter prospect now, however, begins to unfold, and it is surely a pity that it should be obscured by outrages which are a disgrace to a civilised country. Let the frame-breaker remember, that his measures are not calculated to produce any good object — that the poor industrious mechanic falls a victim under the outrage— that measures of this nature are cowardly and cruel — that they are disgraceful and derogatory to the character of Englishmen— and that if persevered in, should he himself escape detection, the manufactory must ultimately be injured, restricted, and ruined,

Nottingham, August 11, 1814.  
AMICUS
```
