The present agitated state of the kingdom, arising from the want of employment, and reduction in the price of that little there is, is seriously alarming. The very heart and extremities of the country have more or less been the scene of popular tumult. Carlisle, Sheffield, Bristol, Leeds, Huddersfield, Truro, and Manchester, have witnessed the most disgraceful scenes of outrage. In most of these places, we are happy to hear, that by the exertions of the magistracy, aided by the promptness of the military, complete order has been restored. The Manchester Mercury of Saturday, says, "We have the satisfaction of assuring our distant readers, that this town has quite resumed its wonted tranquillity, and that no very serious breach of the public peace has occurred since the disgraceful proceedings of Wednesday week." It would have been gratifying to us, could we have added, that the public peace continued uninterrupted during the day in that populous town; but the contrary is the fact. On Saturday, soon after the market began, very large bodies of people congregated together, who manifested dispositions to outrage, and at length their numbers amounted to not less than twelve thousand! Betwixt one and two o'clock, the mob began their depredations upon the meat in the shambles at the New Cross, the whole of which was seized upon by the multitude, and carried off in triumph. The potatoes brought to market shared the same fate, and for some time, the proceedings of the rioters were unresisted. LANCASHIRE RIOTERS. SUBSTANCE OF THE TRIALS. On Tuesday the 26th ult. Abraham Charlson, Job Fletcher, Thomas Kerfoot, and James Smith, were found Guilty of burning the weaving mill, at West Houghton. It appeared by the evidence, that this mill or factory contained neatly 200 pair of looms, which had been worked by means of a steam engine for about four years, but that method was first brought into this county by Sir Richard Arkwright, about thirty years ago. For some time previous to the 24th of April, it had been thought necessary by the proprietors, on account of the disturbances which had taken place near them, that the premises should be protected by a strong military force, which was accordingly pro- cored from Bolton, but it left them on the 22d. - There were 12 stand of arms left in the mill by the Local Militia, to enable the workmen to protect it, and to continue working, which they did till the morning of the 24th, when they were so alarmed, that the superintendent of the business, according to orders he had previously received, stopt the mill, which was deserted by the workmen. A party of the Scots Greys were procured from Bolton, about noon, but on their arrival, finding no appearance of riot or mob, they immediately returned. About four o'clock, the manager, who had continued in the mill, being more alarmed, went to Bolton for the military, which was about four miles from the mill. When he was mounting his horse, having locked the doors, he observed a considerable number of suspicious persons about the mill, several of whom cried out, "Knock him off, kill him, he's going to fetch the Greys" He returned about dusk, and found the mill nearly consumed, only some of the timbers being on fire, and the mob was gone, - As soon as the Greys had left the mill, a number of people gradually assembled till about five o'clock, when the mob came, to the number of near 100, armed with pitchforks, billhooks, scythes laid straight in the handle, and large sticks. They were seen passing through Chowbent, which is about two miles from the factory, between three and four o'clock, and at that time Job Fletcher was among them. Smith, who walked on crutches, headed the mob up to the factory, who immediately began the work of demolition by throwing stones and bricks through the windows, and breaking open the doors. Smith was observed to be very active, hal- lowing and encouraging the others, and throwing stones and whatever he could pick up, through the windows. Charlson was also seen armed with a scythe, walking as sentry before the mill, occasionally throwing at the windows, and cutting down the window-frames with the weapon. There was a cry among the mob, both within and without the building, "Let us set fire to it." Upon which Charlson gave up his scythe to another person, and ran to a barn, belonging to a public house, near the mill; brought out some straw, which he took into the building; upon which they put several pieces of cloth, taken from the looms, upon it, and having procured light from a neighbouring house, set it on fire and in a short time it was entirely consumed - During the time it was burning, the mob continued shouting and making a noise, several expressing their wish that the factory people were all in the blaze. Kerfoot was also observed amongst the mob, shouting and huzzaing, and breaking the windows; he was one who cried out, "Set fire to it." Smith was seen during the fire conducting himself in the same riotous manner, telling them to get on with the fire, and when he saw it could not be extin- guished, said he would be going a little before them, as he was lat of foot, and could not get off the ground as fast as they could. Between one and two o'clock of the same day, Fletcher was met by one of the witnesses, heading 40 or 50 people, near Chowbent, on the contrary side to the factory, and coming along the road; he stopt the witness, and clapt his hand upon his shoulder, saying, "Take notice, that yonder devil is not on fire before three hours are over," at the same time explaining that he meant the West Houghton factory. He was afterwards seen amongst the mob at the time the mill was burnt. A party of the Scots Grays arrived about three quarters of an hour after the roof had fallen in, at which time the mob was dispersed, having had intimation of their coming from persons who were on the watch. The value of the property destroyed was estimated by one of the proprietors at 7 or 8000L. When the evidence for the crown had closed, Mr. Raincock, who was counsel for several of the prisoners, made an objection to the manner in which the indictment was laid, it stating, that the prisoners were charged with setting fire to and burning a weaving- mill, warehouse, or loom-shop, &c. He said, that the word mill evidently meant a place in which corn or any thing else was ground; and it could not be said that any thing was ground in that factory - He also mentioned several other ways in which the word mill was not applicable to the building, and contended, that it was improperly described as a warehouse or loom-shop. He was followed on the same side by Mr. Addison, jun. Mr Park replied at some length to these observations, and the objection was overruled by their Lordships. An attempt was made on behalf of Job Fletcher to prove an alibi, but it did not succeed. Several witnesses gave the prisoners good characters up to the time of this unfortunate affair happening. The next day, Christopher Medcalfe, James Brier- ley, Henry Thwaite, Thomas Pickup, John Hurst (who went by the appellation of General Ludd) Samuel sdsliffe, and Joseph Greenhalgh, were found Guilty of aiding and assisting in the administering of an unlawful oath, to Holland Bowden, a Serjeant in the Bolton Local Militia. It appeared by the evidence, that the Magistrates, having had intimation of unlawful meetings being held near Bolton, had given orders to several persons in the Local Militia there to disguise themselves, and attend them, in order to watch their proceedings, and to report them to the commanding officer. Accordingly a number of them did so, and on the night of Sunday, the 19th April, about eleven o'clock, went to a place called Dean's Moor, where they found near 100 people straggled about, who afterwards formed themselves into a body. Before they got to the main body, they were challenged by a man armed with a pole with a knife at the end of it, and upon some one answering for them, were allowed to pass. There was one man who had the command, and was called General; he had his face whitened, and was armed with something like a Serjeant's halbert in one hand, and a pistol in the other. Whilst they were on the moor, there was an alarm that the horse were coming, and many were running away, but upon the General crying out "Stop and double, there are enough for the horse," about 70 or 80 gathered round him He said, "We will cross over the country to West Houghton, and deceive the soldiers ; cheer on, my lads, there are enough now, and we shall be joined by hundreds before we get there, and they are now waiting for us at Chowbent " Some of the crowd said, as the night was so far advanced, they had better march off as quick as possi- ble. The crowd then marched between two men, of whom the General was one, and were counted as they went through. After marching a little way across the moor, they halted, and formed a rear- guard of as many as were armed. At this time, one of the witnesses asked a man if he thought the attempt would be safe? He was told that two delegates were sent to West Houghton, to get what information they could, as to the place being guarded ; and if it was not guarded, they were to remain there, but if it was, they were to return as quick as possible. They then marched on to a place called Hulton lane ends, where they met Bowden. Greenhalgh said, "He's nothing but a spy, he's a Serjeant in the Bolton Local Militia ; d——n him, I know him, either twist him or puff him;" meaning either swear him in or shoot him. Bowden acknowledged that he was a serjeant in the Bolton Local Militia but not understanding what they meant, he was not very willing to be either twisted or puffed, but he was told he must suffer either one or the other,- or turn back. He said, he could not turn back, for he had a party to take to Manchester at three o'clock, and was going to get his horses ready, and agreed to be twisted. Having got a man to repeat the oath, they ordered him to say it after him, another man holding a hatchet, of something of that kind, over his head, all the time. From what we could collect of the oath, none of the witnesses being able to repeat many of the words, it was nearly as follows: "I solemnly swear, and of my own voluntary will do declare, that I will not discover or reveal the names of the persons who compose this secret committee: I also further declare, under the sacred canopy of Heaven, that I will not inform of, either by word or sign, their person or persons, dress, features, meeting-place or places, under the penalty of being sent out of the world by the first brother who may meet me, never to be remembered but with abhorrence and contempt; and I will, with unceasing vengeance, pursue the traitor to the verge of nature. *Manchester, June 16.*— About twelve o'clock on Saturday, the awful sentence of the law was put into execution upon the eight unfortunate persons condemned at the late Special Assize at Lancaster, viz. Jas. Smiith, Thos. Kerfoot, Job Fletcher, and Abra. Charlson, for burning, &c. Messrs. Wroe and Duncough's Weaving mill, at West Houghton.— John Howarth, John Lee, and Thos. Hoyle, for breaking into the house of John Holland of this town, and stealing bread, cheese, &c.— And Hannah Smith, for committing a highway robbery, by stealing potatoes at Bank-Top, in this town.


---


In *Evans and Ruffy's Farmer's Journal*, [Monday 07 September 1812](https://britishnewspaperarchive.co.uk/viewer/bl/0004607/18120907/028/0006).

A most important cause his been decided at Lancaster Assizes. It was an action brought by Messrs. Wroe and Duncroft against the Hundred of Salford, for the recovery of the amount of their losses sustained by the destruction of their weaving mill at West Houghton, a short time ago, by a mob who set it fire. A verdict was given for the plaintiffs in the full amount of their damages, which were upwards of £7,000.
