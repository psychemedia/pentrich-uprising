Salisbury and Winchester Journal

Published Monday, 14 March 1803

At the Assizes for the county of Wilts, which began here on Monday last, Thomas Hilliker `[sic]`, a workman in the clothing business, was tried before Mr. Baron Thompson, on a capital indictment, charging him with having riotously assembled with divers other persons, and wilfully aided in setting fire to Littleton Mill, in the parish of Steeple Ashton. The trial was an object of great interest to the manufacturers and their workmen, particularly to the shearmen and weavers, the setting fire to the mill having been one of those outrages which arose out the dispute respecting the of machinery, &c. and it was effected by seven men, entered the mill armed, and with their faces blackened, and whilst two of them stood centinel over Mr. Ralph Heath, the Overseer of the Mill, threatening him with instant death if he endeavoured to prevent their purpose, the others went up stairs, and deliberately set fire to the building. A very numerous body of their associates supposed to have been assembled in the neighbourhood, and to have detached these men on this dangerous service.—Messrs. Gibbs and Dallas conducted the prosecution; Mr. Garrow was engaged for the prisoner on a special retainer, with Messrs. Jekyll and Burrough.—Mr. Heath was positive Hilliker was the second who entered the factory; he wis near him a considerable time, so that he had opportunity of observing his whole person; and another witness proved that Mr. Heath, on afterwards seeing Hilliker, recognized him as the man, and was much agitated thereby, Mr. Garrow endeavoured to shew that the men who entered the factory were disguised, it was impossible for any one to know their features; and he cross-examined Mr. Heath, whether he had not confessed that he could not swear to the prisoner. Mr. Heath said, his face was not so black as to hide his features, and that he had said he did not know the prisoner from a dread that his life was in danger, if it had been understood that he could convict him.—The Jury deliberated for about teh minutes, during which time Hilliker was in great agitation; when they at length pronounced him *Guilty*, he appeared as if relieved from a suspense, and to assume degree of fortitude to encounter his fate, and which he retained when sentence of death was pronounced. He is a good looking youth, only 19 years old. He is left for execution.

Six other weavers or shearmen were indicted for unlawfully administering oaths; but though a presumed accomplice was admitted evidence, the charge was not substantiated.

Oracle and the Daily Advertiser

Published Tuesday, 15 March 1803

At Salisbury Assizes, on Wednesday, a cause of great importance to manufacturers was decided. It appeared that Littleton Mill, the property of Mr. Naish, of Trowbridge, had been destroyed by fire on the 21st of July, by a number of riotous workmen. The Counsel on behalf of the landed Gentlemen of the Hundred, endeavonred to prove that the words of the Act, "demolish and pull down" did not include destruction by fire; but the Judge was of opinion, this case was within the meaning of the Act, and the Jury consequently found two verdicts, for the Proprietor and Occupier of the Mill, for the sum of £8000 each, subject to the reduction and amendment of the Counsel, to whom the particular claims of the Plaintiff were referred by the Court. This fine will fall very heavy on the Hundred, which is thinly inhabited.

RESTORATION OF HELLIKER'S TOMB.— During the past week the bath of Thomas Helliker, whose remains are interred in our Parish Churchyard, has been restored. Helliker was a young clothworker of this town, and like many others when machinery was introduced into our factories, thought they were all going to he ruined, and some of them went and set fire to Littleton Mill. Helliker was arrested and at the Wilts Spring Assizes in 1803 he was condemned to death, and hung at Salisbury on the 22nd of March in that year. It is said that Helliker was not the real perpetrator, although he knew, and rather than give evidence which would have saved his own life and condemned others, preferred to suffer himself. Others have said that he confessed after he was condemned, but then it was too late. He was looked upon as a martyr, amid his body was brought to Trowbridge and buried with great ceremomny, the funeral *cortege* being met at some distance out of the town by a procession of cloth workers, and amongst them a number of young girls dressed in white, and headed by a band of music playing solemn dirges The tomb was erected at the time principally at the expense of the "old shearmen" of the town, and was rendered in 1847 when the churchyard was re-laid out. It has fallen to decay lately very much; and its present restoration is mainly to be attributed to the exertions of Mr. Churchwarden Mackay, by subscriptions from the clothworkers in the counties named below. There are two marble slabs let into the panels of the tomb, On one is the following inscription:

"Sacred to the memory of THOMAS HELLIKER, the thread of whose life was cut in the bloom of youth. He exchanged mortality for immortality March 22nd. 1803, in the 19th year of his age.

"The fatal catastrophe which led to this unfortunate event is too awful to describe, suffice it to say that he met death with the greatest fortitude and resignation of mind. Considering his youth he may be said to have but few equals.  He died a true penitent, being anxious in his last moments that others might take a timely warming and avoid evil company.

This tomb was erected at his earnest request by the cloth-making factories of the Counties of York, Wilts, and Somerset as a token of kind love to him and veneration for his memory.

On the other panel is the following:—

"This stone was formerly placed over the remains of Thomas Helliker, who departed this life on the 22nd day March, 1803, Aged 19 years.

"At a time of great disturbance throughout the manufacturing districts of this county, he was condemned for an offence against the law, of which he was afterwards believed to be innocent, and determined rather to die than give testimony which would have saved his own life but forfeited the lives of others.

"Some of the Clothworkers of the town being desirous to perpetuate the remembrance of such an heroic act of self-sacrifice, have restored this memorial in the Year of out Lord, 1872."

The notice of the death of Thomas Helliker and his tomb in Trowbridge parish churchyard last week, has attracted considerable attention to that sad relic of past period, and the desire is increased to thoroughly restore that monument of self-devotion. Trowbridge Chronicle

Published Saturday, 14 May 1881

History of Trowbridge. [BY HISTORICUS.]

THE STORY OF THOMAS HELLIKER.

In the early part of the present century, when the enquiring minds of Stevenson, Brunel, Arkwright, the Heathcotes, and many others, were introducing machinery to supersede hand labor, a great outcry was raised by the artizans who had been accustomed to complete their work by their own ingenuity, that their bread was to be taken from them and that in future men would not be required to finish work which could be executed by mechanical means. So intense was the feeling that many acts of violence were the outcome. In Nottingham and many other large towns whole buildings were wrecked and the machines they contained scattered to the winds.

One class of handicraftsmen in connection with cloth manufacturing was shearmen; a branch of the trade that required peculiar capabilities in the workers, and unique and particular they were in all that pertained to them. For the information of those readers who may not understand the work of "shear-men," we need scarcely say that their duties were to shear the face of the cloth with large appliances answering to that description of implement; and such perfection did they attain in the art of shearing, that a cloth was by that process rendered smooth as satin on the face of it. The work required considerable bodily exertion, and was necessarily a tedious performance. They were a jovial section of the community were the old shearmen, for the beer score of one shop full of men, in one week, reached 1008 quarts! They were very choice, too, in their social intercourse; for a particular part of a public-house was reserved for their accommodation, and woe betide those who pre-occupied it. The admission to the brotherhood was stringent; and, after initiation, each one's interest was identical, no matter what the sacrifice, in defence of what they considered to be their rights. Thus much to explain the terrible event we are about to relate. About 1802 new machinery began to be introduced into the cloth trade. A Mr. Nash, cloth manufacturer, had a mill in the Conigre, and one at Lyttleton, in which machinery had been placed. The shearmen, in a body, with blackened faces, sought to destroy it, first by setting fire to the mill in Trowbridge. Thus emboldened they went to Lyttleton. Amongst the party was a man who had peculiarly projecting front teeth. He carried a horse pistol. Arrived at Lyttleton, the watchman, Heath, was called out, and coolly ordered to clear out himself and family, unless he wished to be burnt alive, as they (the shearmen) were come to burn down the mill and its contents. With the formidable horse pistol presented at his head, Heath was only too glad to comply with their orders, stood off at some distance with his family, and was compelled to watch the destruction of his master's mill, powerless to prevent it. Next morning saw Heath in Trowbridge, in company with the constables, in search of the perpetrators of the previous night's outrage. He could not be mistaken; he should be sure to identify the man who held the pistol to his head, because of his projecting front teeth. In Stilman and Noad's shop he came upon a youth of 18 or 19, named Thomas Helliker, who, unfortunately, had projecting front teeth. "That's the man that held the pistol at me," said Heath, and notwithstanding his earnest protestations to the contrive, he was seized, tried at the Spring Assize at Salisbury, found guilty, and sentenced to death, judge and jury believing the witness, Heath, that the youth Helliker was the real culprit who threatened to shoot him. Helliker was an apprentice, and was what was then known as a "shear colt." Willing or unwilling, he was forced to join the band of midnight marauders, who, with blackened faces, fired the mill at Lyttleton; but he protested to the last that he never had a pistol, nor took any part in the dark deeds of that fatal night; but he knew who the real culprit Was; and he was a man with projecting front teeth. Helliker was taken to execution, the rope placed round his neck, he ascended the steps to the scaffold, and stood beneath the beam. Even at that awful moment he was implored by the High Sheriff to confess who the perpetrators of these deeds were, and he was led off the scaffold with the rope still round his neck, in the hope that he would divulge the names; but he steadily refused to do so, knowing that if he did, his life would not he worth much, so stringent were the rules of this mysterious order. Asserting his innocence he was hung, a sacrifice for the rest. So profound an impression did this tragic event create throughout the order of shearmen in the cloth manufacturing districts, that the shearmen of Wilts, Somerset and Yorkshire assembled, the body was brought from Salisbury, and met outside the town by a large number of young girls dressed all in white, and an immense body of people by whom the deceased was regarded as a martyr—and martyr he certainly was, but it was martyrdom worthy a better cause. The body was interred in the Trowbridge Parish churchyard, with much and we believe sincere lamentation; and a tomb was erected over his remains, surmounted by carved work representing the shearmens' arms, supported on each side by the figures of Justice and Mercy, which upheld an urn.

During the time Helliker was in gaol he wrote several letters to his relations and some verses which were printed by Mr. Sweet, of Trowbridge. Following are literal copies:—

[*A Letter to his Mother.*]

March 13th, 1803.  
ALAS my distressed state! but I thank the Almighty God, I am more composed than I was when I saw my Brothers last. Dear Father and Mother, I wish you and my Brothers and Sisters to come here to see me before the 22d of March. Dear Father and Mother, I have been troubled with a very bad head-ake, but thanks be to the Almighty God, that I think I never found myself better than I have been for these two days past, both in mind and body, and I hope it will continue until the fatal day, then I hope the Lord will receive my departing spirit, I hope that my Brothers will be here, for I have something to say to them, so God Almighty bless you all.

*So no more from your unfortunate Son.*

THOMAS HELLIKER.

March 21st, 1803.

MY dear Brothers, I hope this will be a warning to you all, and consider my untimely fate (being cut off in the prime of life), which I hope will be a warning to all young men like me, and for the love of God Almighty, let my fate be always in your memory, to think that before I knew what life was, caught off to everlasting (I hope) happiness; I thank God I find myself very happy in mind, and I hope the Lord God will receive my soul, so no more,

*Remember my last fate,*

THOMAS HELLIKER.  
[*This he gave to his Brothers in the Prison when he saw them.*]

March 22d, 1803.

DEAR Father and Mother, Brothers and Sisters, and all relations, this is my last night; in the middle of the night I wrote this Letter; give my love to my Uncle Ebsworth, and to all the rest of his family. Dear Father and Mother, I think that some of the principal Shearmen has been the ruin of my body, (but I hope the Lord will receive my soul), and that they will put this on my Tomb-Stone to be a warning to all young men; to a warning against bad advice, and for my Brothers to be good to you both, for it is a bad thing to forget you, when you read this I hope my soul will be at rest forever and ever. Dear Father and Mother, give this to my Brother Joseph, and think of me when he swears, and I hope in God he will leave it off.

*Farewell forever and ever, Yours, &c.*

THOMAS HELLIKER

[*This he wrote the same night after he saw his Relations, and left it for them in his stocking. This was his Birth-day and he was Executed in the same hour he was Born.*]

VERSES

To thee my God and Saviour I,  
By day and night address my cry,  
Vouchsafe my mournful voice to hear,  
To my distress incline thine ear.

For seas of trouble me invade,  
My soul draws nigh to death's cold shade,  
Like one who's strength and hopes are fled,  
They number me among the dead.

Remov'd from friends I sigh alone,  
In a loath'd dungeon laid were none,  
A visit will vouchsafe to me,  
Confin'd past hopes of liberty.

My eyes from weeping never cease,  
They waste, but still my grief increase,  
Yet daily Lord to thee I prais'd,  
With outstretch'd hands invok'd thy aid.

Published Saturday, 2 April 1803
